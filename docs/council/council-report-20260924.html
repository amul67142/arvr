LLM Council · 24 September 2026

Am I on the right path?

Render-driven real-estate sales platform, and the Trident Realty proposal. Five advisors answered independently, reviewed each other anonymously, and a chairman synthesised the verdict.

The verdict

Right path, wrong order. The render-driven approach is correct and the demo already proves most of it. But the proposal sells a capability whose central mechanism — turning a render's mask or geometry into clickable per-flat polygons — has never been executed once. Four of five advisors led with that same finding.

Fix it this week, at your own cost, on synthetic assets you generate yourself, before a rupee of the client's render budget is committed. Make camera + proxy projection the primary hotspot technique and Cryptomatte the fallback, with manual tracing as the declared Plan C so no render delivery can ever produce a dead end. Strip token collection out of Phase 2 into a later, separately priced phase. And before pricing anything, ask Trident two questions the proposal never asks: what is your launch date, and which CRM do you already use?

The one thing to do first

Build the hotspot pipeline this week against a synthetic scene you make yourself: model a crude tower, render one beauty frame plus an object-ID pass, export the camera, and make one specific balcony on one specific floor clickable in the existing viewer, end to end. That single clickable balcony de-risks the whole engagement — and it is a better proposal than the proposal.

Agreed, 4 of 5

  • The mask-to-polygon converter does not exist — this is the existential gap.
  • Validate on synthetic assets before the client commissions anything.
  • The pilot batch is a paragraph, not a tested procedure.
  • Token payment collection is liability without proportionate revenue, for now.

Genuine clashes

  • Payments: lock-in (Expansionist) versus relationship-ending risk (everyone else). They disagree on timing, not on direction.
  • Project versus product: the productisation instinct is right, and premature by exactly one successful delivery.
  • Primary hotspot technique: masks, or camera + proxy projection.

Blind spots the peer review caught

  • Manual tracing is a Plan C that always works. A designer can trace 13–20 stop frames in hours. The automation is an efficiency play, not a prerequisite — which defuses the whole "what if it doesn't work" fear.
  • Camera + proxy should probably be primary. The studio already has per-unit geometry; exporting the camera path and a box-per-flat proxy gives exact hotspots at every frame with no EXR parsing and no anti-aliasing gaps between adjacent flats. Cryptomatte was built for compositing, not region extraction.
  • The client's calendar rules. Nobody asked Trident's launch date, RERA deadline, or whether this is a competitive bid.
  • Indian market mechanics. Tokens usually move by cheque or RTGS into a designated account; developers already run Sell.do / LeadSquared / In4Velocity, which block inventory and generate cost sheets; the buyer is reached through relationships, not documents.
  • Founder capacity is the likeliest failure, not the pipeline. If Phase 1 slips, renders are never ordered and Phase 2 never happens.

The advisors, in full

The Contrarian what will fail

The mask pipeline does not exist. There is zero code that reads a Cryptomatte EXR, decodes an Object-ID pass, or converts a mask into polygon hotspots. The render brief tells the studio a script converts every mask into clean outlines automatically. That script has not been written. The only outline code (build-horizon.mjs) does luminance-gradient edge detection on drone footage — a different technique that cannot parse Cryptomatte data.

The pilot batch is the only safety net and it is too vague. If it arrived tomorrow he would be writing the converter under time pressure — debugging EXR parsing (Cryptomatte encodes object hashes in float channels), anti-aliasing artefacts at mask boundaries, and whether V-Ray, Corona and Redshift produce compatible output. Weeks of R&D, not a day's validation.

The payment scope is reckless: no backend, no database, no auth today, yet Phase 2 needs atomic booking with concurrency locks, webhook verification, refunds, RERA audit trails and PCI-adjacent security — while simultaneously building the mask pipeline, stop frames, inventory and salesman mode. Build the converter this week against synthetic masks made in Blender. Strip payments to Phase 3. Every hard unsolved problem is stacked into one delivery window, and when one slips they all slip.

The First Principles Thinker what are we solving

He is asking the wrong question. Not "am I on the right path" but "what am I actually selling, and do I control it?" What he sells is a render pipeline, not software — the React app, the viewer, the inventory engine are commodities. The differentiation is that renders go in and interactive experiences come out. But the converter that justifies asking a client to spend lakhs on Cryptomatte passes does not exist. There is a document describing it.

The single existential question is whether he can take a mask, extract regions, convert them to screen-space polygons and overlay them on the matching orbit frame, reliably, at production quality. Everything else is known-solvable. Stop writing proposals; build the pipeline this week on synthetic renders, then show the client a demo where you click a specific balcony and it responds. That demo is the proposal.

Drop payments: a solo founder adding PCI, RERA, refunds and double-booking prevention to a property transaction for a gateway integration fee is an absurd liability-to-revenue ratio.

The Expansionist the upside being missed

This is a platform play disguised as a client engagement, and he is underpricing what he has. The demo already does what Panom charges lakhs per project for. Trident is the first deployment of a product, not the destination. Thousands of RERA-registered projects launch annually; incumbents charge per-project SaaS fees he could undercut while earning multiples of a services contract.

The render dependency is a moat: once the pipeline works with one studio, it is a repeatable integration pattern. Package it and studios become channel partners who recommend the platform because it makes their renders interactive. Payments are not scope creep but lock-in — once a booking closes through the system, switching cost is infinite, and every competitor demo without a Book Now button looks like a toy.

Price Trident as a flagship deployment with a per-project recurring licence. Phase 1 near cost to get in fast; the money is in Phase 2 recurring and every subsequent project at configuration pricing. Then sign two more developers on the case study.

The Outsider fresh eyes

He is selling a two-phase project where Phase 2 is the entire point, yet Phase 2 cannot be priced, cannot be scheduled, and depends on assets he does not control. The client sees a clear Phase 1 website — every agency sells those — and a Phase 2 that says "trust us, the magic works, but your studio must produce a very specific deliverable we have never processed from a real client." The pilot batch idea is good but buried in an appendix the decision-maker will never read.

The gap is not "will the renders arrive" but "will he know what to do with them when they do." And a home buyer would ask: why can I not just watch a video walkthrough on YouTube? The proposal never answers that. The immersive case is obvious to him and invisible to the person signing the cheque.

The Executor Monday morning

One thing to prove, one thing to cut.

Prove: before any renders are commissioned, build a script that takes a single test Object-ID or Cryptomatte image, runs connected-component extraction (OpenCV, or sharp plus flood-fill), names each region, and outputs the polygon JSON the existing viewer already consumes. Test with a mock — an old stop frame with masks, or a fake one with flat colour regions. Pixel-accurate clickable outlines within a week, or he knows before anyone spends money.

Cut: payment collection. Even with money settling to the developer's account, he still writes the webhook handler, the atomic status flip and the receipt generator. Let the salesman block the unit and generate the cost sheet, then hand off — 90% of the wow factor, none of the liability.

Three gates before ordering renders: pipeline test on a dummy frame; artist confirms Cryptomatte or Object-ID with named objects; pilot batch processed end to end. Request the FBX camera alongside the masks as option B insurance.

Peer review highlights

Which response was strongest, and why

Three of five reviewers picked the Contrarian for naming the exact technical gap — no mask-ingestion code, Cryptomatte's float-channel hashes, renderer incompatibility, anti-aliasing at boundaries — and connecting it to the real schedule risk. Two picked the First Principles Thinker for the more useful framing: what am I selling, and do I control it?

Which response had the biggest blind spot

All five reviewers named the Expansionist. Its strategy is sound in a world where the pipeline already works; it assumes exactly the thing that is unproven. "File the IP" on code that has never ingested a single mask frame was the line that drew the most fire. The upside case survives — but only after one delivery proves the mechanism.

What every advisor missed

Manual tracing as an always-works fallback; camera + proxy projection as the better primary technique; the client's launch calendar and possible competing bids; how Indian developers actually collect tokens and which CRM already blocks their inventory; and the founder's own capacity across three concurrent projects — which the capacity reviewer called the highest-probability failure mode of all.
